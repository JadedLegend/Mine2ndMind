A Place That Never Felt Like Home

I remember one of many nights at a social gathering—one of those times where I walked in with hope, feeling outgoing and extroverted, determined to connect with people. I was in a good headspace, ready to listen, share, and maybe even make new friends.

This place, Adobe, seemed like the perfect spot to meet like-minded individuals. I went there over a dozen times, attending different events, and each time, I made an effort to engage with the community.

I would approach people, congratulate them on their art, or ask them about their passions. I listened intently as they spoke excitedly for a minute or two, often carrying the conversation myself by asking thoughtful questions: \*What inspires you? What obstacles have you faced? What fulfills you about your work?\*

But after a brief exchange, the awkward silence would come. I’d shift the focus, trying to share my own projects and music. Yet, within a minute, almost without fail, they’d find an excuse to leave the conversation.

The Pattern of Rejection

It happened time and time again. I followed countless pages, tried to stay in touch, but I gained no followers, no friends, no connections.

It seemed like everyone already had their group of friends. It reinforced this lingering belief I’ve held for most of my life: that I’m an outcast, and that’s where I belong.

I’ve often felt like an autistic person—but with an ironic twist. Social cues aren’t a mystery to me. I read people easily, even the most subtle ones. I sense the discomfort, the disinterest, the unspoken reasons why the conversation ends.

What’s strange is that the people I gravitate toward—the ones I connect with best—are usually other outcasts. Those sitting alone, looking at their phones, clearly out of place, just like me.

Two Stories, Two Lessons

Two interactions stand out from all those visits to Adobe.

One was with a younger guy at the bar, playing a PC MMO FPS. I carefully tried to strike up a conversation, respecting his focus. Our interaction stretched over ten minutes, but it was essentially just a two-minute exchange dragged out.

Finally, he snapped at me: “Thanks a lot, asshole. Since you’ve been bugging me, my team lost. I’m a competitive pro player.”

His words hit me like a gut punch. I timidly walked away, berating myself for hours. Why did this hurt so much? Looking back, I see it now: it was a recreation of my childhood trauma. The belittlement, the blame for things I had nothing to do with—it all mirrored how my dad treated me growing up.

The second memory is brighter. A young man was there alone, celebrating his 18th birthday. We struck up a conversation, and for nearly an hour, we talked about his life, his hopes, and his dreams.

Eventually, we went to my car, where I smoked him out and gave him some herb as a birthday gift. He didn’t try to talk to anyone else at the event. Like me, he was just there, quietly being ignored.

Those are the people I connect with—the outcasts, the overlooked.

A Pattern That Persists

I don’t connect with people who seem "normal." I can’t. It’s always the ones who share a sense of disconnection, whether from culture, society, or their own struggles, that I bond with.

One exception was a random Facebook friend I recognized at Adobe—a tarot reader and metaphysical polymath. I can’t recall his name, but his energy was different. He belonged in that space in a way I didn’t.

It feels like people who think similarly to me are often celebrated in these subcultures. Yet, for some reason, I remain on the outskirts, unable to find footing, even among those who should be my people.
