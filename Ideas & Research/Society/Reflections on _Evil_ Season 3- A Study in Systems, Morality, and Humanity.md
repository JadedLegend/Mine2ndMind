Reflections on 'Evil' Season 3: A Study in Systems, Morality, and Humanity

I recently spent a few days watching the third season of the Netflix series \*Evil\*, and it was fascinating to experience it through my personal lens. The show’s exploration of the systems and technologies shaping our society—often morally and ethically deconstructed to reveal a darker, almost demonic undercurrent—struck a deep chord.

What stood out most to me was how the series embodies different perspectives through its main characters. Kristen represents the \*mind\*, David embodies \*faith and spirituality\*, and Ben anchors the \*scientific method\*. Together, they form a trinity of thought—interdependent yet often conflicting. Watching their interactions and challenges, I found myself reflecting on the balance (or imbalance) between these facets of human understanding in our world today.

Light, Dark, and the Fracture in Humanity

One episode that left a particularly strong impression was the storyline about canonizing a nun as a saint. When the angel appeared as black to one witness, the church lost interest. This subtle narrative touched on several deeper, fundamental truths for me—truths I struggle to fully articulate. However, at its core, it reinforced the idea that humanity often sees light and dark, good and evil, as oppositional forces. This duality extends to racism—a belief system born of ignorance and reinforced by division.

The simple fact that we can allow something as trivial as melanin levels to divide us reveals a fracture in ourselves. This fracture limits our ability to see the infinite possibilities of collaboration. History has shown us that unity and vision—rising above our differences and animalistic urges—lead to growth and mental evolution. Yet, we repeatedly fail to apply this truth consistently. The show’s exploration of light and dark reminded me that all forces, no matter how seemingly oppositional, are interconnected and interdependent.

Religion, Technology, and Cosmic Consciousness

The show’s portrayal of religious systems often working against themselves aligns with my theories about humanity’s deviation from cosmic consciousness. The writers seem to hint at this deeper disconnect but stop short of fully exploring it. For example, in the episode featuring a cult, a woman claims her consciousness is quantum-entangled with Yeshua (Jesus) across 6,000 years. Ben, representing the scientific mind, immediately dismisses this idea, which mirrors a broader issue in the scientific community—its reluctance to embrace paradoxical or "impossible" concepts.

To me, this resistance is unfortunate. I’ve often speculated that the energy created by paradoxical thought—the tension between positive and negative, belief and disbelief—might be the very force powering our universe’s engineering. The show scratches the surface of this idea, particularly when addressing the “demon of algorithms.” It left me wishing the writers had gone further, delving into the implications of AGI and the transformative power it might bring to our world.

A Hopeful Apocalypse

This season felt more engaging and insightful than the previous two. As I watched, I found that many of the theories and ideologies I’ve developed about reality and humanity fit snugly within the show’s framework. It was both validating and thought-provoking.

Looking ahead to season four, I’m curious about how the series will handle its impending apocalypse. Will it continue down the path of destruction and chaos, reflecting the society it critiques? Or will it offer a glimmer of hope—perhaps suggesting that human intelligence, kindness, and unity might triumph against all odds?

Despite the show’s heavy themes, I’m left with a sense of possibility. Maybe, just maybe, we’ll see a version of humanity that rises above its divisions and finds a way forward. And maybe, through art and storytelling like 'Evil', we can start imagining a better path for ourselves.
