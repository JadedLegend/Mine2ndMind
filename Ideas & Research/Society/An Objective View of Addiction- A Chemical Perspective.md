An Objective View of Addiction: A Chemical Perspective

No chemical is inherently good or bad—it simply \*is.\* It exists without moral weight or agenda. The human mind, however, is obsessed with categorization, with labeling, with assigning meaning. And in addiction, we see a reflection of something far deeper—our fear of losing control, our tendency to demonize what forces us to confront the parts of ourselves we do not wish to see.

The Weaponization of Addiction

Addiction has long been criminalized—not necessarily because of its harm, but because of what it magnifies. It exposes the cracks in the system. It reveals truths that society would rather keep buried. Not to mention its profitability—the War on Drugs emerged just as for-profit prison models took hold in the U.S. Coincidence?

Drugs are a convenient scapegoat. Addiction and drug abuse strip people of their autonomy, reducing them to survival mode, to a cycle of seeking and suffering. And yet, people turn to substances not simply to indulge, but to cope—to numb pain, to quell anxiety, to silence an internal war that modern medicine, with all its profitability, fails to resolve.

People lost in addiction become easy targets—walking cautionary tales, the embodiment of "bad choices." Their struggles are glaring, their dysfunctions obvious. Unlike the more polished addictions society normalizes—consumerism, pharmaceutical dependence, digital escapism—drug addiction is "undeniable." And so, it is condemned, rather than understood.

But if our systems truly existed to "help" rather than \*exploit,\* addiction—and 90% of organized crime—would be drastically reduced. Instead, it serves as a tool of division and control, keeping populations fearful, distracted, and easily manipulated.

Addiction as a Mirror

Addiction forces us to confront our own vulnerabilities. It highlights our compulsions, our escapism, our deep-seated need for connection in a world that denies it.

Instead of addressing the root causes of suffering, society criminalizes addiction, reinforcing cycles of incarceration, shame, and social exile. Rehabilitation centers, institutions that should exist for healing, often operate as exploitative systems—profit-driven, focused on churn rather than transformation. Many seeking recovery are led down close-but-inauthentic paths, given surface-level solutions that fail to address the core of their struggle.

The True Meaning of Recovery

The word "recovery" means "a return to a normal state of health, mind, or strength." But in a world this deeply unbalanced, what exactly is "normal"?

Our society itself is addicted—to power, to consumption, to artificial stimulation. If anything, it is not just individuals struggling with substances who need recovery. \*Society itself is in need of desperate rehabilitation.\*

A Chemical Reality

The human experience is largely a chemical one. Every thought, every emotion, every action is shaped by biochemical interactions. Yet, our relationship with chemicals has become distorted.

We vilify some substances while glorifying others. Pharmaceutical drugs—often synthetic derivatives of naturally occurring compounds—are praised as "treatments," while plant-based substances with healing potential are criminalized. What is the difference, other than control?

Modern society is deeply chemically dependent. From prescription medications to food additives, from caffeine to synthetic mood stabilizers, we have rewritten our own biology. And yet, the same substances that alter our consciousness are either marketed to us or condemned—depending on who profits from their distribution.

The Systemic Dependence on Chemicals

This is not accidental. The systems that govern us—economic, political, and corporate—thrive on dependency. A population in chemical imbalance is a population more easily manipulated. The very attempt to "restore balance"—to achieve homeostasis—is disrupted by external forces that \*profit\* from keeping people sick, distracted, and divided.

Beyond "Good" and "Bad" Chemicals

No chemical is intrinsically good or bad. Only its application, intent, and interaction with human biology determine its effect.

This is evident in the observer effect—a principle in quantum mechanics that suggests the act of observation changes the observed reality. The same principle applies here: a chemical in itself is neutral, but its impact shifts based on how and why it is used.

Some chemicals heal. Some harm. Some sustain life, others alter it beyond recognition. The interplay between chemistry and consciousness is undeniable. To truly understand addiction, we must acknowledge that we are not just victims of substances—we are participants in an intricate biochemical dance, one that extends beyond human experience and into the very nature of existence.

The Influence of Subconscious Programming

We like to believe in free will. I mean, we have 127 flavors of artificially sweetened drinks and 246 variations of corn-based snacks at every gas station—total freedom, right? But how much of our behavior is truly self-determined? How much is simply the result of subconscious programming, external manipulation, and chemical imbalance?

Most people don’t choose addiction. Paradoxically, I feel like I did.

At 17, my goal was clear: do as many drugs as possible and sell even more. I chased it recklessly, becoming chemically dependent on nearly everything addictive. I spent five years—one of them behind bars for armed home invasion—fully immersed in that world, with zero motivation to change.

And then, I was violently shaken awake by a spiritual experience that lasted for months.

That was over 15 years ago. Since then, I’ve learned a lot—about myself, about society, about reality itself. I don’t claim to know anything for certain, but I know this: addiction is not a moral failing. It is not a lack of willpower.

It is a symptom of deeper programming—patterns etched into the subconscious long before a person ever takes their first hit, drink, or pill. The first seven years of life shape the foundation of a person’s mind, and by adulthood, approximately 95% of our daily actions, thoughts, and behaviors are controlled by this subconscious blueprint. It is a system built from early experiences, environmental influences, and societal conditioning—many of which we had no say in.

And this programming isn’t static—it is reinforced. The more a person engages in the same behaviors, the deeper those neural pathways become. Addiction doesn’t just alter brain chemistry; it cements a feedback loop. A cycle that, without intervention, feeds itself.

If the subconscious has been programmed for self-sabotage, self-destruction, and the pursuit of temporary relief over long-term healing—how can we expect people to operate differently?

Yet, the mind is not set in stone. It can be rewritten. Just as a computer can be updated, so too can the subconscious. The key is interruption—consciously breaking the cycle, disrupting old patterns, and carving new pathways. Many who embark on a journey of self-reconstruction—whether through meditation, therapy, or entheogenic medicines—discover that healing is not about willpower alone. It’s about systematically rewiring how the mind processes reality itself.

The Fractured Human Psyche

People speak of "zombies" in modern society—those glued to their phones, those lost in addiction, those numbed by escapism. But these are not symptoms of individual weakness—they are reflections of a collective illness.

The unbalanced psyche of the individual is a fractal of the unbalanced psyche of society.

Addiction—whether to substances, technology, or materialism—acts as a magnifying glass for the traits we most want to deny in ourselves. It makes for an easy villain, yet in reality, it is a symptom of far deeper systemic fractures.

A New Definition of Recovery

So what does it mean to recover?

It means more than simply getting "clean." It means more than abstaining from substances.

True recovery is a return to balance. It is the process of unlearning what was imposed upon us and remembering what was lost.

It is not just individuals who need recovery—\*it is society itself.\* Until we recognize this, we will continue to treat addiction as an isolated issue, rather than what it truly is: a symptom of a much larger imbalance.

If we want to heal, we must redefine what healing looks like.

If we want freedom, we must first understand what chains bind us.

And if we truly seek recovery—then we must be willing to confront the truth, no matter how uncomfortable it may be.

If we refuse to question the systems that shape us, are we truly choosing at all?

The question isn't whether recovery is possible—the question is, will we recognize the need before it's too late?
