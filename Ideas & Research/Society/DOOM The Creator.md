I just saw this photo of Earl Sweatshirt and Tyler, the Creator watching DOOM front row, and I’m honestly enamored. The looks on their faces—pure amazement and wonder—captured in the presence of an absolute legend. DOOM, the godfather of abstract lyricism and multisyllabic rhyme patterning, carved his own lane entirely. Though, honestly, if we're talking about the \*true\* originator of multisyllabic flows, that might go to CL Smooth.

There’s something magnetic about DOOM. What attracts me—and so much of the culture—to him is his fearlessness to be different. He wasn’t afraid to be abstract, weird, or even downright jarring. His catalog is a reflection of that: some pieces are beautiful diamonds, others are straight-up “WTF is this?” But that's what made him so singular. He took risks, and not everything landed perfectly, but when it did? It transcended.

I can’t say the same connection exists for me with Tyler or Earl’s music. I’ve probably heard only 3-5% of their catalogs. While I respect their artistry, it hasn’t ever drawn me in deeply. On the other hand, I’ve listened to over 90% of DOOM’s vast catalog. His work resonates on a different level, perhaps because of how unpolished and human it feels. It’s not trying to cater to anyone—it just \*is\*.

Personally, I’ve always been more of a TDE fan than Odd Future. Schoolboy Q, Ab-Soul, Jay Rock, SZA, Zacari, Reason, Lance Skiiiwalker—they’ve always held more substance for me. Even Mac Miller, who I associate more with TDE than Rostrum, evolved in a way that few saw coming. One minute, he’s rapping about playgrounds and high school antics, and the next, he’s dropping \*Watching Movies with the Sound Off\*. That album floored me. It felt like he grew up overnight into something profound, something magnificent.

Odd Future, despite my outward appearance and vibe, never really resonated with me. I’ve always felt like they leaned into the “jackass label of hip-hop,” and while that’s cool for what it is, it just didn’t hold my attention. Tyler’s greater works have always been hit-or-miss for me. I appreciate his creativity, but parts of his music have felt more chaotic than cohesive to me. Maybe that’s the point.

I wish I lived somewhere with a more active music scene. I dream of finding a live band to work with or collaborating with like-minded people to bring ideas to life. But no one ever seems to stop and help me, despite how often I stop to help others. Whenever it’s physically possible—no matter who it is—I step up. That’s my mission in life: to help ease the weight that sits on every human’s shoulders. I know that weight too well, and I feel a responsibility to make it lighter for others whenever I can.

There’s a bittersweetness to this realization, though. It feels like unrequited love—giving so much and rarely feeling that energy returned. I’m far from perfect; I lash out sometimes, especially at those closest to me, when I collapse under the pressure. The weight society forces us to carry is immense, and sometimes it breaks through in moments I regret.

But what keeps me going is seeing artists evolve—truly evolve. It’s rare. Too often, I’ve seen artists find one “flavor” that works and then try to replicate it endlessly, thinking that’s the key to success. It’s not. Growth is the key. Watching someone like DOOM, or even Mac in his later years, push the boundaries of what they could be—that’s inspiring.

Art should always strive for more, to reach into the unknown. That’s what makes it timeless. And maybe that’s why that photo of Earl and Tyler resonates so deeply with me: because it’s a reminder of what it means to witness greatness, to stand in awe of someone who never stopped evolving.
