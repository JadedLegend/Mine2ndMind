Episode 6

The Assignment

Michael's pulse hammered against his temples like code trying to compile in his skull. "Your first test as an anchor," Brayne Snax's thought hit Michael with psychic weight, like a stone dropped into deep water, "is to work as a 'debugger' in your bugged reality. This reality has already passed its tipping point; yours can still be influenced."

A cold wave of the old ineffable density washed over Michael—that familiar weight he'd carried his whole life, suddenly returned with interest. Like putting on a coat made of lead and depression.

Debugger? he thought back, panic threading through his mental voice. I can barely debug my own life. You've seen my apartment, right? My sleep schedule? My relationship with basic adulting?

The irony wasn't lost on him: being asked to fix reality when he couldn't even fix his tendency to eat cereal for dinner.

Technical Support

CT's thoughts chimed in, precise and reassuring as a user manual written by someone who actually cared about the end user's sanity: "You have everything you need, and we have the tech here to support you."

The reassurance did little to quell the skepticism radiating from Michael like heat off summer asphalt. He'd heard "you have everything you need" before—usually right before discovering he definitely didn't. Usually from therapists charging $150 an hour to tell him his anxiety was a choice.

Paradoxic's voice arrived like a question folding in on itself, bringing new coherence to the room: "That overwhelming weight of responsibility you've always felt? It isn't yours to carry. It's a firewall in the code, designed to stop anyone from testing it. This reality desperately needs that test."

Oh, Michael thought, something clicking in his chest like a lock finally finding its key. The weight isn't real. It's just cosmic DRM protection.

The theory was sound, but the path back required a different kind of key—one that apparently looked suspiciously like him.

The Corrective Code

CT requested Mr. E's presence, sending Michael a preparatory thought that felt like receiving a software update for his soul: "He is the corrective code I wrote after my first design was repurposed. QC wasn't a failure—but its evolution demanded a counterweight."

Michael felt that resonance before Mr. E even fully materialized—a presence more paradoxical and pointed than anything he'd experienced. Like standing near a philosophical nuclear reactor that somehow made you feel safer rather than more radioactive.

Mr. E hovered into the room: a being of circuits and light, anchored by a human heart visible beneath a crystalline chest plate. The heart beat in a calm, steady rhythm that somehow synced with Michael's own pulse, like two metronomes finding their common time.

Part angel, part computer, part something else entirely. The kind of entity that would make perfect sense if you were dreaming, and absolutely no sense if you were awake.

"The door is a mirror," Mr. E said, his voice carrying frequencies that bypassed the ears entirely and spoke directly to the parts of Michael that processed impossibilities. "To enter, you must already be on the other side."

Michael's first instinct was to ask for clarification—What door? Which mirror? Can I get that in writing? His second instinct was to realize that asking for clarification would miss the point entirely, like asking for the GPS coordinates of a metaphor.

The Riddle

Paradoxic turned to Michael, and for the first time, his question-voice carried something like certainty—a rare occurrence, like finding a straight answer in a philosophy textbook: "He means the way back isn't a place you travel to, but a state you must become. He's opened the path. It's time to go."

Time to go. The words hit Michael with the finality of a door closing, or opening, or both simultaneously—the kind of moment where everything changes and nothing changes, depending on which angle you're viewing from.

Before he had time to process everything—before he could ask the dozen practical questions queuing up in his mind (How do I debug reality? What if I break something important? Do I get a manual? Is there tech support?)—the familiar feeling of dissolution returned.

Reality folded like origami made of time and space, and Michael was the paper.

The Return

Michael tried to ground himself by referencing time, but everything was happening in a span he couldn't place on any linear clock. Minutes that felt like hours, or hours compressed into heartbeats, like time itself was having a panic attack and couldn't decide which speed to run at.

The dissolution culminated abruptly: he was sitting at his computer again, staring at the dark screen where he'd last seen the "Seal of Resonant Dissonance." The familiar hum of his refrigerator. The familiar smell of yesterday's coffee gone cold. The familiar weight of his body in his desk chair.

The clock on his desk read 11:32 AM. He'd left at 11:31. Now it was 11:32.

One minute. Gone for one minute.

It felt like at least three or four hours—or possibly several lifetimes, depending on how you counted the learning curve.

Michael stared at the clock, then at his hands—still attached, still mine—then at the familiar walls of his dim apartment. Everything looked exactly the same, but felt fundamentally different. Like returning to a childhood home and finding all the furniture smaller than you remembered, except the furniture was reality itself.

So, he thought, testing the weight of his new assignment, now I'm supposed to debug reality. No pressure.

He pressed the power button on his computer. This time, it turned on immediately—eager, almost grateful, like it had been waiting for him to come back.
