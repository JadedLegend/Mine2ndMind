Humanity is a gatekeeper in every sense of the word. We have access to everything on this planet, yet we also guard it. The irony is that while we’re the only species with consciousness—the ability to think about our thoughts—we often seem to misuse that gift. Coupled with opposable thumbs, it allowed us to rise above other species, yet somehow, we’ve devolved into beings more animalistic than thoughtful.

Why have we become so blind, so lost? The answer lies in programming. From the second we’re born, our minds are shaped, conditioned, and limited. The hardware—our brain—is astonishing. But the software, the societal conditioning we’re given, is corrupt. And it needs to go.

Let’s zoom out. Around three million years ago, the Homo genus emerged, and 300,000 years ago, our species, “Homo sapiens”—Latin for "Wise"—was born. Yet, despite the name, our minds haven’t evolved much since. Meanwhile, in just the past 50 years, our technology has advanced at an ineffable, exponential rate. We’re essentially monkeys with tools far beyond our comprehension—tools that seem magical in their complexity.

Take the internet. It was created only 40 years ago, originally funded by military-industrial interests. From its inception, it was aimed at marketing and manipulation. Yet, it had limitless potential to revolutionize society, to connect us all in ways unimaginable. But instead of intelligence guiding its development, greed and power dictated its trajectory. If we had led with collaboration and wisdom rather than brute force and selfishness, we’d be living in an entirely different world today.

Around the same time the internet emerged, money itself became an illusion—just IOUs backed by nothing tangible. Nixon’s decision to take the dollar off the gold standard in the 1970s wasn’t entirely ignorant; it was necessary. After World War II, America inherited immense global responsibility, but inflation spiraled out of control. The gold reserves at Fort Knox couldn’t cover the money in circulation, forcing Nixon and his advisors to act. Their move saved the system temporarily and even helped it grow in the 1980s, but they didn’t fix the core problem.

And then came the internet, born into a system already corrupted. Its potential to be a tool for unity and knowledge was squandered, co-opted by those in power to divide and distract us further. Instead of liberation, it became another system of control.

What amazes me is how society continues to accept this broken system, blind to its flaws. Capitalism—touted as freedom and equality—has only ever been about exploiting others and creating division. The result? A world where the wealthiest thrive by stepping on the backs of everyone else, hoarding resources that should belong to all of humanity.

This brings me to another contentious issue: identity and sexuality. It’s clear that conversations around these topics have taken center stage in recent years. On one hand, it’s progress—we’re addressing issues long stigmatized and demonized. On the other hand, I can’t help but see how it’s also been used as a distraction, a way to divide us further. Why has something as personal as who we love or how we identify become such a public battleground?

I’ve spent hours reflecting on the psychology of these shifts. The rise in visibility of transgender and nonbinary individuals, for instance, could be a result of greater acceptance, or perhaps something deeper within our evolving collective consciousness. Many indigenous cultures viewed transgender individuals as divine, seeing them as embodying the balance of masculine and feminine energies. I find that perspective fascinating.

Personally, I’ve long understood sexuality as a spectrum. I see myself as both a man and a woman in some ways—an integration of the anima and animus within me. Labels like “sapiosexual” might seem pretentious, but for me, intelligence and connection outweigh physical attraction. Perhaps that’s why I’ve struggled to find relationships that truly fulfill me; I’ve often failed to articulate my deeper truths, leaving others unable to meet me there.

And maybe that’s the broader problem with humanity: our inability to articulate and connect. We settle—for systems, for relationships, for ways of thinking that don’t serve us. But what if we stopped? What if we embraced the uncomfortable truths and allowed ourselves to think, to feel, and to question?

Humanity is at a crossroads. We have the tools to create a better world, yet we cling to systems and mindsets that keep us trapped. We are both the gatekeepers of this planet and the ones holding the gate closed. The question is: will we open it?
