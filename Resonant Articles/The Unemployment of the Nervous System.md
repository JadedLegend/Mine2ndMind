The Unemployment of the Nervous System

(a personal essay by Michael Mistree)

I. Prelude: The Myth of Functionality

I have been told for as long as I can remember that stability comes from structure, that a job is both proof and anchor. But my nervous system has always read “structure” as threat. My body doesn’t trust clocks. It doesn’t believe in alarms. It hears routine not as safety, but as captivity.

It’s not rebellion; it’s physics. The smallest demand for sustained performance ignites the same circuitry that others reserve for emergency. The office, the schedule, the task list, all appear to my body as a subtle form of warfare.

II. The Somatic Loop

There is a perpetual hum under my skin, anticipation without target. When I’m told to “relax,” my system interprets that as “let your guard down and be caught.” The freeze-response masquerades as procrastination, the flight-response as distraction. I do not lack motivation; I lack the physiological permission to act without panic.

Work requires a nervous system that can cycle between activation and rest. Mine is stuck in activation - or collapse. The workday doesn’t map to my biology; my biology was trained by crisis, uncertainty, and environments that punished stillness.

III. Psychology: The Trajectory of Effort

Somewhere along the way, effort became indistinguishable from exposure. Every attempt to “try” carries the memory of humiliation - being told it wasn’t enough, or that my pace was too strange, or that my intensity was excessive.

So now, when effort appears, my psyche pre-emptively aborts it. Why begin what will end in rejection? Why raise the head that history keeps cutting down?

The culture rewards steady output. My mind works in floods and droughts, bursts of visionary synthesis, then silence. The labor market calls that inconsistency. I call it metabolism.

IV. Society: The Machinery and the Flesh

The modern economy is built for predictable nervous systems, ones that can tolerate monotony, dissociation, and hierarchy. It wants “self-regulating units of productivity.”

My nervous system is relational, responsive, allergic to abstraction. It thrives on meaning, context, and improvisation. It dies in systems that mistake compliance for competence.

So it isn’t that I “can’t hold a job.” It’s that most jobs require a form of self-abandonment that my body can no longer perform.

V. Mythic Underlayer: The Exile and the Gift

Every system pathologizes the nervous system it can’t use. But history is full of misfit nervous systems that became artists, inventors, healers, edge-walkers.

Perhaps my task isn’t to fit the machine, but to remember the rhythm it forgot. My sensitivity, my oscillation, my aversion to forced routine, these aren’t malfunctions; they are instruments tuned to a different field.

The tragedy is not that I cannot work; it’s that the world refuses to value the work I was designed to do: to listen between frequencies, to notice the quiet ruptures where truth leaks through.

VI. Coda: Toward a Humane Economy

If labor were measured not by endurance but by coherence, not by hours but by depth of presence. I would be thriving.

Until then, I survive in fragments: small bursts of creation, long silences of recovery. Each day, I try to build a life that honors my nervous system’s wisdom instead of punishing it.

It’s not resistance. It’s realism.

The machine requires sameness; I am built for aliveness.
