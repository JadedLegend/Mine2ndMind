Episode 9

Michael's body hummed with contradictions. So this was it, he thought, perhaps tasting his first true moment of calm since reality had begun bending under his hands a week ago. Of course emotional resonance was the hack. He'd read about "law of attraction" for years, understood it intellectually. This was that principle but running on the universe's actual operating system.

He tested something small. Sitting at his desk, he focused on the tea cup on his bookshelf—the one that had been gathering dust for months. Felt the frequency of what it would be like already resting in his palm. The weight of ceramic, the warmth of tea that didn't exist yet.

And—there it was.

His hand closed around nothing, but the cup materialized inside his grip like it had always been there. Steam rose from tea that smelled like chamomile and possibility.

Then a startle broke the frequency. The glass liquefied between his fingers like melted honey, and scalding tea splashed across his palm. He yelped, more shocked by the failure than the burn. The liquid ceramic bled through his fingers onto his jeans.

"Shit," he muttered, shaking his hand. The burn wasn't bad, but his pride was scorched.

Then Brayne Snax's presence boomed into his skull, thick as molasses and twice as smug.

Reality-hacking teacups, Mikey? Real classy. Why not rob a bank while you're at it?

Michael's neurons fired at the suggestion—his body involuntarily tagging it as a "good idea" before his rational mind could object. The chemical rush of excitement made him dizzy.

Brayne's psychic laughter rolled through him like distant thunder.

We've discussed what the most efficient move is. We've decided on a communal reversion.

Michael blinked, still nursing his burned hand under cold water from his bathroom sink. "Communal reversion?" He tried to parse the words. "Is this about those cultural inversion theories I just wrote?"

Exactly. We want you to revert your community from externalized worship to internalized.

Michael frowned. "I don't even have a community. I can barely manifest a teacup without melting it. How am I supposed to reprogram a whole group of people?"

That's the genius of inversions, Mikey. The coding is automatic. But you still have to hold the frequency. And you have to decide if you're ready for the cost.

The room seemed to shimmer slightly, like reality was holding its breath.

\---

Michael closed his eyes and tuned to the idea. Internalized worship. Not bowing to idols or groveling before external power, but honoring the divine spark inside each person. The sacred self, not the inflated ego. He held the resonance in his chest like a tuning fork, tried to let it hum outward through whatever invisible networks connected him to the people who read his scattered thoughts online.

The air thickened like a shared breath. For a moment, he felt it: a soft inward turning across the collective field, a remembering of something forgotten.

Then his phone started buzzing like an angry wasp.

Notifications cascaded down his screen: friends, acquaintances, strangers he'd never heard of quoting phrases from posts he'd half-forgotten. Except they weren't using them to look inward—they were wearing them like designer labels.

"I am divine ✨" someone hashtagged, posting a mirror selfie with ring lights and filters.

"Worship the god I am 😍🔥" another declared, followed by ten pouty faces and a link to their OnlyFans.

"Internal worship means I deserve everything 💅" accompanied by a shopping haul video.

Michael's gut twisted like he'd swallowed broken glass. They weren't honoring themselves—they were inflating themselves. The inversion had inverted again, like a snake eating its own tail.

Brayne's laugh cracked through his skull.

Careful what you code, Mikey. Words don't mean what you think they mean once they hit the collective unconscious. You asked for worship turned inward, and look—everyone's bowing to their own reflections.

Michael groaned, sinking into his desk chair. "I don't have a community. I have a feedback loop of narcissism."

\---

But as he scrolled through the digital wreckage, scanning the endless mirror selfies and half-baked manifestos, threads of something else glimmered between the noise.

A stranger in São Paulo quoting him word for word, adding their own thoughtful commentary about the paradox of seeking without grasping.

A group chat he didn't recognize—maybe twelve people—debating his theories about paradox like they were sacred texts, picking apart the implications with the kind of care usually reserved for scripture.

A handful of names that kept appearing in his notifications, not with selfies or slogans, but with questions. Real questions. The kind that showed someone had actually read past the surface.

Recognition sparked in his chest. Fragments. They weren't a coherent community, not yet—but they were orbiting the same ideas, drawn to the same frequency. Some of them had been quietly engaging with his work for years, leaving comments that made him think, sharing his posts with friends who might understand.

The responsibility landed on him like a lead blanket. He didn't want this. Not after almost fracturing his timeline over a diabetes cure, not after burning his hand on liquefied ceramic. He was barely holding himself together with paradox and stubbornness. Now he was supposed to anchor others?

"Wrong move," he muttered, watching another influencer claim divine status while selling supplements. "I should've kept the theory locked in my notebooks."

Wrong, Brayne thundered, his psychic voice carrying the weight of cosmic certainty. You have fragments. Readers. Strays who stumbled into your frequency and didn't look away. The question isn't whether you have a community. The question is whether you'll claim it—or keep pretending you're alone while people build altars to your ideas.

Michael stared at the glow of his screen, torn between awe and dread. The vanity spiral was still spreading like a virus, but somewhere inside it pulsed a quieter signal—people who weren't performing or posturing, but actually listening. Actually trying to understand.

The seed had already been planted. The frequency was already transmitting.

The only question was whether he would water it, or let it grow wild.
