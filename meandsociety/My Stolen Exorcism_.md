I was pouring a drink just now, and a random thought hit me—something I haven’t reflected on in years. Back when I was in Peru for a month, drinking ayahuasca, I feel like I had an exorcism \*stolen\* from me. Let me explain.

This happened during the 2 months I spent deep in the Amazon rainforest. Communing with the sacred medicine Ayahuasca. It was during the 4th or 5th ceremony out of eight. Each of us had the opportunity to sit with the shaman and have an icaro specifically sung for us, with its energy directed toward our individual healing. When it was my turn, things got... interesting. I accidentally kicked over my puke bucket. Some other ineffable things happened that I can’t really describe—too abstract for words.

But then, something even crazier happened. While I was receiving my icaro, one of the other participants—Craig, a cool guy from Australia—went into what can only be described as a full-blown exorcism. His body was contorting in unnatural ways, he was making bizarre noises, and several people had to hold him down.

It was unlike anything I’d ever seen.

When we talked afterward, it was clear that something dark, something heavy, had left him. You could see the shift in him—it was palpable. Whatever that negative presence was, it was gone. And honestly? I was happy for him. I truly was. But I also couldn’t shake this gnawing feeling of envy.

It wasn’t directed at him, per se. It was more directed towards the spirit of ayahuasca itself, as if it had chosen him over me. For years, I think I carried a subtle resentment toward ayahuasca for what I perceived as “missing my chance.” I \*wanted\* that exorcism. I thought I \*needed\* it. I can’t even fully explain why, but I did.

Craig’s life seemed to change drastically after that. Mine did too, but only for a while. The ayahuasca helped me, undeniably so—for about three to six months. But as usual, when I came back home, the lack of a community led me to slowly disintegrate again.

That’s why the iboga community meant so much to me later on. I’d see them monthly, helping with ceremonies, and occasionally having my own ceremonies for free. Toward the end, they even started paying me. It was the closest I’d come to a real sense of belonging. But, as it tends to go, people you look up to don’t always live up to the picture you’ve built of them. I feel deeply let down by my iboga fam.

Now, I have no community. Facebook feels like my only one, and honestly, it’s mostly just me talking to myself. Sometimes I worry that some of my better writings here will get lost in the void, but at this point, I don’t really care.

One of the hardest feelings to sit with, for me, is envy. It doesn’t come often, but when it does, it carries a weight that feels almost... guilty. It’s a rare thing for me to envy someone else’s accomplishments—normally, I’m genuinely happy to see people succeed. But in this case, with Craig and the exorcism, I couldn’t help it. For years, I felt like I’d been overlooked, like the healing I so desperately needed had been handed to someone else.

It sounds crazy, I know. But try sitting in my body for two minutes before judging my writings. I’ve often wondered what it would be like to truly see the world through someone else’s eyes. To feel what they feel. It’s a thought that’s always fascinated me.

That envious feeling I had back then? It’s similar to the feeling I get when I see people with, like, 5/100 talent blow up while I sit here with no audience. It’s not a good feeling. But at least I can recognize it for what it is and try to let it go.

At the end of the day, it’s all part of the journey, I guess.
