Had some 12-step zombie try and, I don’t know, hurt my feelings? Telling me, “Oh, you sound like someone who’s failed out of AA a few times, har har.”

Failed out of AA? More like was forced to go to meetings starting at age 11. Then forced some more. And more after that.

Eventually, I tried it my way. That didn’t work out, but it sure broadened my perspective.

Afterward, in a desperate attempt to have my dad in my life, I went back to the rooms. For four years, I tried my absolute best to do exactly what I was told. All the while, something felt off. It didn’t look or feel right. There was some untruth lurking in plain sight.

So I researched it. Deeply. I read books, scientific journals, and even explored through psychedelics. What I found were many truths—my truths—about my experience. No one can take that from me.

Here’s what I discovered: \*AA is a sham. The 12 steps? A cult.\*

It’s taken many people I’ve cared about and completely erased their identities. Sure, they might be sober—but if they’re miserable, what’s the point? Or worse, if their entire life revolves around “recovery,” then what kind of life is that?

Yes, there’s a small percentage of people—maybe 5%—who seem to figure out a way to make it work for them. Good for them. But even then, they have to overlook and ignore the glaring inconsistencies in the rooms.

And what about the old-timers? The ones everyone looks up to? Let’s talk about their high suicide rate. Most of them spend decades living a lie, trading one unhealthy obsession for another.

Then there’s the saddest group: the "recovery addicts.” These are the people who have no personality or identity left. Everything they do is by the book—\*the\* book. They’re not living; they’re existing as an extension of the steps. It’s honestly heartbreaking.

Did you know that addiction remission happens naturally? Harvard medical studies show that it’s about the same percentage as people who manage other chronic conditions like heart disease or diabetes. And that’s without AA.

What’s even worse is that AA’s complete-abstinence model can do more harm than good. There’s no focus on harm reduction, even though they know the vast majority of people—over 95%—don’t stick around.

And don’t get me started on the humiliation and fear tactics. Those are straight-up cult methods. Sure, AA might not have the physical abuse of more extreme cults, but the psychological manipulation is the same.

The allergy myth? That one kills me. People still quote it from the Big Book, even though it’s been thoroughly debunked. It’s laughable.

Did you know that Bill Wilson, the guy who started it all, thought LSD should be part of the 12th step? Or that he begged for whiskey on his deathbed?

AA’s rise to prominence wasn’t even based on success—it was thanks to a woman who testified to Congress about how it was the only path to sobriety. She wasn’t even sober when she said it.

And let’s talk about the predatory behavior in the rooms. Sure, not every group is like that, but read \*The Orange Papers\* if you don’t believe me. Decentralization means every group is different, but it also means there’s no accountability. I’ve seen exploiters protected by the group simply because they stopped drinking, even though they were sexually preying on newcomers. It’s sick.

AA protects predators and shames victims. It’s a breeding ground for dysfunction.

I could go on forever. But at the end of the day, my experiences—and the truths I’ve uncovered—speak for themselves.
