Free climbing has always fascinated me in so many ways. There’s something so raw and primal about it—a pure test of skill, strength, and mental focus. No ropes, no safety nets, just you and the rock. It doesn’t matter how experienced you are, how many peaks you’ve conquered, or how much preparation you’ve done; it only takes one mistake. That’s the unforgiving reality of free climbing. Most free climbers don’t live long. And most climbers, no matter how adventurous, don’t dare to free climb.

One of the most iconic free climbs ever attempted was on El Capitan, the legendary 2,900-foot granite monolith in Yosemite National Park. Alex Honnold’s free solo ascent of El Capitan in 2017 brought the sport into the spotlight, widely covered by National Geographic and immortalized in the Oscar-winning documentary \*Free Solo.\* That’s when free climbing first entered my awareness, and since then, I’ve been hooked.

I’ve watched countless documentaries and devoured a few books on the subject. The stories of these climbers—their discipline, their fearlessness, and their connection to nature—are mesmerizing. There’s a part of me that longs to climb myself. Even with ropes and harnesses, I think scaling some of those walls would be an incredible experience. But free climbing takes it to a whole new level. Listening to climbers describe how they feel while scaling these walls—how they become one with the rock, how they feel part of the mountain, part of nature itself—there’s something spiritual about it.

Most free climbers meticulously plan their climbs. They’ll ascend the same route multiple times with ropes, memorizing every handhold, every foothold, every move. And then there are the outliers, the daredevils who push the boundaries even further. I watched a documentary about a climber who lived this philosophy to the fullest—a man who didn’t just dabble in free climbing but made it his way of life. He was one of the first to summit multiple peaks in Patagonia, often without ropes. What truly set him apart, though, was that he didn’t just climb rock. He free climbed ice. Sheer, unforgiving ice.

His approach to life was as daring as his climbs. He lived simply, out of a van, traveling wherever his climbing dreams took him. He had a partner who shared his passion, a girlfriend who climbed alongside him. He was a free spirit in every sense of the word. Sadly, like so many others in this extreme sport, his story ended too soon. But the mark he left on the climbing world and the inspiration he gave to others remain timeless.

Climbing, especially free climbing, is right up my alley. It’s a dance with gravity, a razor-thin line between life and death. The stakes couldn’t be higher, but neither could the rewards. To conquer not just the rock, but your own fear—that’s what makes it so captivating. For now, I can only dream about it. The nearest climbing gym is three hours away, and the rocks around here aren’t exactly Yosemite. But maybe someday, I’ll stand at the base of a towering wall, hands chalked, heart pounding, and start the climb. Until then, I’ll keep watching, reading, and marveling at these incredible athletes who redefine what it means to live on the edge.
