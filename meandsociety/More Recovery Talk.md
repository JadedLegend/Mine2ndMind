There’s a saying in recovery programs: “You have to be broken down before you can be built back up.” The problem is, the ones doing the breaking and rebuilding are often unqualified, egotistical narcissists. I’m not saying this about everyone, but after years of personal experience, I’ve yet to see much evidence to the contrary.

It’s painfully obvious how many sponsors, those supposed beacons of guidance, seem to revel in their control over others. I’ve seen extreme introverts forced into leading meetings, their discomfort dismissed as "growth." I’ve watched sponsees pushed into prisons, detoxes, and psych wards to recruit people when they’re at their lowest, using vulnerability as a tool for conversion. The power dynamics in these spaces aren’t just toxic—they’re predatory.

For years, I clung to the 12 steps because I was told it was the answer. I desperately wanted to reconnect with my father, and the program seemed like the only path forward. But I’ve come to realize: the steps didn’t save me—they almost destroyed me. The system isn’t designed to foster growth or healing; it’s designed to perpetuate itself.

I finished the steps. I did the work. And yet, I felt no relief. No miracle. Just dread. They told me to get a sponsee, to “give back,” but I wasn’t ready. Still, I was pressured into it, handed rules that boiled down to: “Stick to the Big Book—it’s gospel.” Except, it wasn’t my gospel.

The program’s reliance on forced vulnerability, negative self-talk, and outdated dogma causes more harm than healing. People are told to "own their part" in horrific traumas, like abuse or assault. They’re made to believe their pain is somehow their fault. It’s disgusting, and it’s damaging.

The stats speak for themselves. Less than 0.05% of newcomers ever make it to 15 years sober. That’s not a success rate—it’s a churn rate. Spontaneous remission has better odds than the 12 steps. And yet, the program is treated as the \*only\* path to recovery. Why? Because it’s institutionalized, protected by anonymity, and above scrutiny.

Anonymity in the 12 steps feels like a shield for predators and a way to strip people of their identity. It’s in the very definition: \*having no outstanding, individual, or unusual features; unremarkable or impersonal.\* The program asks people to relinquish their sense of self, to conform, to become anonymous. But what’s recovery if you lose your identity in the process?

The community aspect of the 12 steps is its only redeeming quality. Humans need connection, and shared experience can be powerful. But the problem is the community itself. So many meetings are filled with people who are emotionally unhealthy, judgmental, or stuck in cycles of superiority complexes. I’ve seen it time and time again—cliques, ego trips, and toxic dynamics that make the space feel more like high school than healing.

Recovery isn’t a one-size-fits-all solution. Abstinence-only models don’t work for everyone, and science backs that up. People need individualized care, professional trauma counseling, and evidence-based treatment methods. Programs should empower people, not break them down further.

The real tragedy is how outdated our approach to addiction is as a society. We criminalize it, stigmatize it, and profit from it. We could solve so much—reduce organized crime, improve public health, and save countless lives—if we treated addiction as a public health issue instead of a moral failing. Decriminalize drugs. Regulate their sale. Provide education, support, and access to harm reduction. It’s not a radical idea; it’s common sense.

But common sense doesn’t seem to drive policy. Profit and control do. And so we remain stuck in this broken system, perpetuating harm while pretending it’s the only way forward.
