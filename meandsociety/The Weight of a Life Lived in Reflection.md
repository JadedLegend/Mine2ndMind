The Weight of a Life Lived in Reflection

I think most people have always been this annoying. Not everyone, of course, but the vast majority. I’ve internalized so much anger throughout my life—more than I can fully grasp. Letting some of it out now has been revealing, almost like opening a pressure valve I didn’t realize was about to explode.

The other day, I was venting to my mom. She tried to calm me down, as she always does. I stopped her and said, \*"I’m sorry if this makes you uncomfortable, but I can’t and don’t want to internalize my anger anymore."\* She paused for a moment and said, \*"I’m sorry. That’s my problem. I always feel the need to fix everything."\*

It hit me then: I’ve been carrying this weight my entire life, letting the pressure build until it blows. Maybe I’ve truly “blown up” 1-2 dozen times in my life, but it’s never felt like enough to release what’s inside.

The Roots of My Anger

I believe my tendency to internalize everything—especially anger and hurt—began in childhood, with my dad. I’ve spent most of my life allowing people to walk all over me, and it’s only now that I understand why.

My dad has always acted as though he didn’t want anything to do with me. Straight up. I know when people are lying or putting on a front—I’ve always had that sense—and my dad was the king of it.

When I turned 18, I didn’t speak to him for five years. Why? Because he tried to keep me in a miserable program where I’d already spent three years. My mom had to bring the police to get me out on my 18th birthday because my dad showed up, acting threatening and calling her an idiot.

He never supported me—not emotionally, not financially, not in any meaningful way. Sure, he paid for that program, happy to spend $70-100k a year to keep me out of the way. But beyond that? Nothing. No investment in me, my growth, or my future.

After five years of silence, I tried to reconnect. I moved to New Mexico to build a relationship with him, but nothing changed. He wouldn’t even loan me $50 for a gym membership, despite knowing I’d been going daily for three months. That was the most I’d ever accomplished physically, and I never got back to that point.

He always found ways to belittle me, to make me feel small. He never respected me, never seemed interested in spending time with me. Even after years of trying to earn his approval, I realized it was a losing battle.

The Cost of Seeking Connection

I spent years trying to build a relationship with my dad, thinking it would heal some part of me. But the truth is, he’s never taken responsibility for the ways he’s hurt me.

I remember the one time he did a 9th Step with me—his “amends” after 24 years of sobriety. It was the most insincere, hollow apology I’ve ever heard. It was clear he wasn’t interested in repairing anything.

Eventually, I stopped trying. I sent him a text a few years ago, expressing my feelings, and he responded days later, denying everything. That was it. I’ve sent seven messages since then, attempting to reconnect, but he hasn’t responded. Maybe he changed his number. Maybe he’s ignoring me. Either way, I’ve come to believe he’s relieved to no longer have to deal with me.

The Weight Lifted

These realizations have been painful, but they’ve also brought clarity. I’ve stopped internalizing everything, and I’ve started speaking my mind. I’ve told more people to fuck off in the past month than I have in my entire life. I walk away from nonsense conversations, call people out when they’re wrong, and if they prove me wrong, I apologize.

For the first time, I feel a sense of liberation. It’s not anger for anger’s sake—it’s reclaiming the parts of myself that have been suppressed for decades. I’m done bending over backward for people who don’t deserve it.

The Search for Community

I’m still searching for a community, a group of people who resonate with my values and experiences. I thought I found it in Orlando, with the iboga group, but I was let down. I’ve tried 12-step programs, but the steps feel like basic kindergarten morality wrapped in pseudoscience.

The only thing 12-step programs offer is community, but even that is flawed. The people I’ve met in those rooms often seemed more interested in power and control than genuine connection. The steps themselves are outdated, and the success rate is abysmal.

I don’t fit the normal boxes, and I’m okay with that. But finding people who truly understand me has been a lifelong struggle. I know I’m not crazy—the world is. And I refuse to conform to a broken system.

Moving Forward

Writing and music have been my salvation. They’re the only things, aside from benzos, that help ease the physical tension of my anxiety. I’ve always been an open book, unafraid to share my life with anyone interested. But I’ve reached a point where I won’t waste my energy on people who don’t value it.
