Medication Response Summary

Over time I’ve noticed a consistent pattern in how my system responds to psychotropics. Stimulants (methylphenidate or amphetamine class) bring me into what I call actionable coherence — a state of functional alignment, improved executive sequencing, and a sense of self-directed agency. They don’t feel euphoric so much as clarifying; my cognition organizes and external demands become navigable.

In contrast, most antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, and atypicals) create an experience of disconnection from self and environment — emotional blunting, flattened affect, loss of intuitive processing, and a sense of “observing life through glass.” These effects outweigh any mood benefit. Sedating agents (mirtazapine, trazodone, etc.) induce dissociative half-dream states rather than restorative sleep.

Seroquel (low dose) has been the one partial exception — its antihistaminic effect allowed deeper sleep without major identity distortion, though discontinuation produced transient “electric-shock” sensations.

Overall, my nervous system appears to respond best to agents that enhance coherence and executive function rather than those that dampen or globally modulate neurotransmission. My goal isn’t stimulation but functional integration — maintaining clear consciousness while regulating physiological overactivation.
