A Personal Account of Suspected AuDHD, PDA, and Functional Collapse

I want to be clear up front that I am not attached to labels as identities. I’m using terms like AuDHD and PDA because they are currently the closest language I’ve found for describing patterns that have been present across my life, especially in how my nervous system responds to demands, stimulation, and structure.

For most of my life, my internal experience has been characterized by high cognitive activity paired with inconsistent functional access. I don’t experience myself as unmotivated or indifferent. I experience myself as blocked, often unpredictably, especially when something becomes framed as an obligation rather than a choice.

Executive Function and Demand Avoidance

Executive dysfunction has been one of the most impairing aspects of my experience. This isn’t limited to procrastination or distraction — it often feels like a hard stop between intention and action. I can understand what needs to be done, want to do it, and still find myself unable to initiate or sustain the task.

When demands increase — especially social, administrative, or externally imposed ones — I notice a strong internal resistance that doesn’t feel willful. It feels more like a threat response. My body reacts before my reasoning can engage. This aligns closely with descriptions of Pathological Demand Avoidance (PDA), particularly the anxiety-driven avoidance rather than oppositional behavior.

Over time, repeated failures to meet expectations — despite genuine effort — led to shame, withdrawal, and eventually agoraphobic patterns. Leaving my home, committing to schedules, or engaging in systems with unclear or shifting demands became increasingly difficult.

Sensory and Cognitive Load

I also experience heightened sensitivity to sensory and cognitive input. Environments that are noisy, unpredictable, or socially dense drain me rapidly. At the same time, understimulation leads to restlessness, rumination, and difficulty regulating attention. I often feel caught between overwhelm and under-arousal, with little stable middle ground.

This internal contradiction is exhausting, but I don’t experience it as chaos. It feels more like a nervous system tuned for a different environment than the one I’m in.

Effect of Stimulant Medication

When stimulant medication was introduced, something very important happened:

many of the barriers I’d assumed were psychological or motivational quieted enough for me to function.

Executive tasks became accessible.

Leaving the house stopped feeling dangerous.

My world expanded instead of shrinking.

The most striking change was that agoraphobia and paralysis dissolved, not through effort or therapy alone, but through improved neurological regulation. This strongly suggested to me that these symptoms were not primarily characterological or anxiety-based, but downstream effects of dysregulated executive function.

When the medication was later removed, those barriers returned — not because I had “lost progress,” but because the underlying access to action and regulation was gone again.

Identity, Masking, and Misinterpretation

Throughout my life, I’ve learned to mask — to appear more consistent, capable, and emotionally regulated than I often feel. This has helped me survive socially, but it has also led to repeated misinterpretations: that I am choosing not to engage, that I’m resistant, that I’m intelligent but lazy, or that my struggles are situational rather than systemic.

Internally, I experience myself as highly perceptive, thoughtful, and engaged, but limited by an unreliable interface between intention and execution. I’ve done a lot of self-reflection already; what I’m hoping for now is help stabilizing and expanding my capacity, rather than continuing to search for explanations I’ve already spent years exploring.

Why I’m Seeking Collaborative Care

I’m not looking for a single label or a one-size-fits-all solution. I’m looking for collaborative exploration of how my nervous system works, what supports actually improve access and stability, and how to reduce the cycle of burnout, withdrawal, and collapse.

I’ve been keeping an emotional and experiential journal for months, and I’ve collected informal materials (including memes and descriptions from others with similar profiles) not because I want to self-diagnose rigidly, but because pattern recognition has been my most reliable tool.

My goal is not to be “fixed,” but to be supported in a way that allows consistency, autonomy, and dignity.
