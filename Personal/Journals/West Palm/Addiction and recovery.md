Addiction and recovery —

two words I know too well. Two words I’ve been pressed into like a pair of gloves, told they must fit for the rest of my life: either in active addiction or in recovery. I resent that binary. I’m not sure whether the idea is too simple or too tangled; probably both.

From where I stand, addiction and recovery feed off one another, each fuelling the other, keeping us locked in a sick, looping state. Once in a while people wrench themselves out of that cycle. I believe that cycle has been strengthened — even sold — by the monetization of so-called “recovery.” With that thought (and knowing it could be wrong), I’ve been looking for an exit from the circuit of fear, pain, and shame.

The truth I don’t want to say aloud is also the truth I keep seeing: doing the uncomfortable things slowly rewires me. Small exposures to discomfort make me more comfortable in the long run, and the reverse is true as well. It’s a stubborn, paradoxical law — the thing that undoes us is sometimes the very lever that frees us.

For years I was dragged to AA meetings as if punishment — starting when I was thirteen. Over the past fifteen years I tried it here and there without really grasping what I was after. I can’t claim I found some final answer, but right now, for the most part, I’m happier and more able to choose constructively instead of destructively. That shift came from two things I found in twelve-step rooms.

First, fellowship — the awkward, pushing-through-the-door kind — which felt excruciating at first but slowly became sustaining. Second, the practice of leaning into discomfort until the body and mind stop reflexively fleeing it. Neither was glamorous. Both were ordinary, stubborn, and true.

The second are the steps themselves, which I missed the point of the first few times around.

My understanding of the steps this time around is that they are quite literally a spiritual toolkit, which can be used to apply principles to every part of life.

If I do my best to practice these principles in all of my affairs, my general state of mind and actions will improve. So that’s what I plan on continuing to do

I relapsed many times. The drugs offered brief relief, and then none; the old wiring would reassert itself and I’d watch my life as if from a distance, trapped in misery and powerlessness. Sometimes only a crisis — overdose, forced rehab, collapse — interrupted it long enough for me to try another path. I tried many paths. Some offered partial solace; none rewired me completely. Often I lacked the discipline to sit through the initial tidal wave of emotional pain that true change requires.

And yet — I stayed. I kept trying. Those small, brutal steps added up. The transformation I wanted arrived not as revelation but as slow accretion: discomfort accepted, choices

rebuilt, a life made more whole.
