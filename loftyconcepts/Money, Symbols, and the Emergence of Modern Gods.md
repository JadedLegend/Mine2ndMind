Money, Symbols, and the Emergence of Modern Gods

Symbols—whether Masonic, Templar, or those found in corporate logos—may represent more than human creativity. They could be universal signposts, reflecting the archetypes and structures embedded in the collective unconscious. These symbols, far from arbitrary, carry power because they resonate with deeper truths about humanity’s spiritual and material journey.

Nowhere is this more evident than in the story of money. In the 20th century, we simultaneously devalued our dollar and deified our currency. By untethering money from natural resources—gold, land, or anything intrinsically valuable—we severed its connection to the tangible world. Money transformed from a tool for exchange into an abstract concept, capable of infinite expansion and manipulation. In doing so, we laid the groundwork for mega-corporations and the rise of economic gods.

Corporations as Superhuman Entities

The decision to grant corporations legal rights—equating them to "persons"—was a seismic shift. The idea was simple: groups of people working together would make better, more ethical decisions than individuals acting alone. But this reasoning overlooked a critical truth: collective focus creates entities. Just as ancient gods were born from the shared worship of human minds, corporations became living, breathing forces.

These corporate entities were not merely groups of people making decisions; they became superhuman beings—entities with singular purposes, driven by profit and perpetuation. They lacked morality, empathy, or foresight beyond their own survival. In essence, humanity created gods, and those gods now rule us.

Like the monotheistic God that dominates much of modern spirituality, money became the ultimate symbol of power. With money, humanity could achieve godlike feats: transform landscapes, build empires, conquer diseases, and bend time itself through technology. Yet the pursuit of money often comes at a devastating cost—demanding not just labor but the very essence of one's soul.

The Illusion of Scarcity in a World of Abundance

This deification of money occurred during a period of rapid technological evolution. New technologies offered the promise of abundance, efficiency, and global connection. Yet, instead of ushering in an age of prosperity for all, they created a cascade of wealth concentrated in the hands of a few.

The economic systems humanity built were rooted in the illusion of scarcity—an idea that resources are limited and must be hoarded to ensure survival. But technology shattered that illusion. We now live in a world where scarcity is artificially maintained to uphold the systems that profit from it. The gods of money and corporations perpetuate this scarcity, despite existing in a reality capable of abundance.

Selling Souls to Serve the System

In this world, money has become both the carrot and the stick. With enough of it, you can transcend the limitations of the human condition, achieving the kind of influence and power once reserved for deities. But to obtain it, most people must participate in systems that demand conformity, exploitation, and compromise.

This is the paradox of modern existence: to succeed in the world, you often have to sell your soul—sacrificing authenticity, creativity, and integrity to serve a system that thrives on disconnection. The very structures we’ve created to empower humanity have become the chains that bind us.

A New Kind of Awakening

What’s fascinating is that humanity is beginning to see these patterns. The very tools created by corporations—like the internet—are now being used to expose their flaws. We are collectively recognizing that the gods we’ve created are not benevolent. They reflect our shadows: greed, fear, and the desire for control.

But this awareness also opens the door to transformation. If we understand that money, corporations, and even historical symbols like those of Freemasonry are constructs—powerful, yes, but ultimately malleable—we can begin to reclaim our agency.

Symbols can be rewritten, systems can be restructured, and gods can be replaced. It requires the same collective focus that gave rise to these entities in the first place—but this time, directed toward balance, equity, and shared humanity.

The question is: can we rise to the challenge? Or will the gods we’ve created continue to shape our reality, unchecked and unquestioned?
