The human brain doesn't develop logic first. It develops emotion first.

Our limbic system — the architecture of fear, attachment, loyalty, threat — comes online years before the prefrontal cortex fully matures. The parts of us that reason, weigh consequence, and hold long-term complexity don't consolidate until the mid-twenties. We are feeling creatures long before we are thinking ones.

And consider the context in which that wiring takes shape. For roughly the first seven years of life, a human being is almost entirely dependent on their immediate environment for survival, for meaning, for their foundational model of how the world works. Whatever that environment teaches about scarcity, trust, threat, and belonging becomes the operating system — long before the individual ever gets to consciously choose their beliefs.

So the arc of individual development tends to follow a recognizable pattern: total dependence, then separation and individuation — the painful work of becoming a distinct self — then a long stretch of navigating emotional impulse and reactivity, and only later, if conditions allow, a kind of integration where instinct and reason begin to work together rather than against each other. That integration is where depth lives. That's where wisdom becomes possible. Now here's the thing worth seeing: that pattern isn't only individual. It's fractal. You can map it onto the species.

Humanity as a whole has been moving through these same developmental stages — long periods of dependence on systems we didn't choose, painful individuation across cultures and eras, centuries of reactive and impulsive organizing, and only now approaching the threshold where genuine integration becomes possible. We are still early in that process.

The fact that every dominant system to date — capitalist, communist, fascist, theocratic — has relied on exploitation as a primary mechanism doesn't mean exploitation is the only engine available. It means we haven't matured past it yet. These are not the final architectures. They are developmental stages, each one reflecting the level of integration the species had access to at the time.

The question isn't whether people, as they are right now, would show up for something better. The question is whether it's possible to build systems that don't require human beings to be ground down in order for the thing to function — and whether the architecture of what's possible is changing faster than the stories we tell ourselves about what's fixed.

I think it is. Not because people are inherently good or bad, but because the developmental trajectory hasn't stopped. The capacity for integration — individual and collective — is still willing to actually practice.
