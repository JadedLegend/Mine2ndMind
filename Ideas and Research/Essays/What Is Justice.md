Bridges Not Cages: On Justice and Truth - Forces That Shape Us

Prelude: A Question, Not a Claim

We often speak of truth and justice as if they are fixed stars—immutable, eternal, known.  
But what if they’re rivers?  
What if they’re mirrors?  
What if they’re alive, breathing with us, changing as we change?

We live in a world that demands certainty.  
But beneath the noise, what many of us long for is resonance: a sense that something is aligned, real, coherent.  
Not perfect, just real.

And so, we begin not with declarations, but with questions.

What is True Justice? Who Decides?

Justice has been a cornerstone of civilization since the beginning.  
Ancient cultures created laws to keep order. Babylon was the first to codify them, but with brutal consequences. A son striking his father might lose his hands.

Since then, justice has evolved.  
English common law introduced precedent. The Magna Carta limited royal power. The U.S. Constitution enshrined rights.  
But despite its evolution, justice still echoes the same question:  
What is truly just?  
And who gets to decide?

The Flaws in the Modern System

Law today feels diluted. Contradictory. Full of loopholes, prejudice, and bias.  
A trap for some.  
A golden key for others.

Like everything else, justice has been commodified.  
A sprawling “justice industrial complex” intersects with the prison industrial complex, both backed by the full weight of the U.S. government.

In the U.S., federal judges are appointed, not elected, and no formal qualifications are required.  
That’s flexibility in theory.  
In practice? It opens doors to ideology, bias, and corporate interests.

The Rise of Mass Incarceration

Since the 1970s, the U.S. incarceration rate has exploded. In the early part of the decade, it hovered around 160 people per 100,000. But over the next three decades, it surged—quadrupling by the early 2000s, until more than 2 million people were locked away. That’s nearly 1% of the U.S. population at any given time. The number has plateaued in recent years, but we still imprison more people per capita than any other developed nation.

And this didn’t happen because crime dramatically increased. It happened because policy did.

Mandatory minimums. “Three strikes” laws. The War on Drugs. All shaped not by crime rates, but by fear-based politics and tough-on-crime rhetoric, almost purely in the pursuit of profit.

In 1984, the first private prison contract was signed.

Since then, the logic of incarceration has quietly but deeply shifted. Prisons, once a public burden, became a private business. And like any business, it needed growth. It needed quotas. It needed “customers.” The criminal legal system, already riddled with bias, became even more extractive. And communities of color, the poor, and the mentally ill became pipelines for profit.

Once profit is involved, justice is no longer the point. The goal becomes expansion. Repeat offenders, longer sentences, harsher penalties, not because they serve the public good, but because they serve the bottom line.

A System Hijacked by Greed

Today, justice is less about fairness and more about control.  
Money and power tilt the scales.  
Judges, laws, and prosecutors are often shaped by corporate hands.

For every corruption story that surfaces, dozens stay hidden, buried in bureaucracy or silenced by fear.

And the real threats?  
The ones profiting off pain?  
They walk free.

The War on Drugs: A Missed Opportunity

Addiction isn’t a crime.  
It’s a condition, trauma, neurochemical imbalance, some unmet need.

But instead of healing the root, we criminalize the symptoms.  
We demonize substances that could be medicine for some, when used with respect.

The stated goal is always “protection.”  
But the effect has been paradoxical - causing more harm, not less.

We say we want health.  
But we choose punishment.

330 million people, many dependent on government employment, all hopelessly entangled in and dependent upon hierarchy within hierarchy.  
Each layer protects its core, promising change while delivering its opposite.

If we invested in healing instead of harm, we could dismantle criminal networks from the inside out.  
But we don’t.  
Because healing doesn’t feed the machine. Control does.

Capitalism and the Inversion of Justice

Our justice system, just like everything in a consumption-based culture, has become inverted through its commodification.

Prisons generate profit. Court fees prop up bureaucracy. Lobbyists write laws. Corporations script morality.

Ask yourself:  
What happens to the economy if we only detain 100,000 people instead of 3 million?

How many jobs vanish?  
How much revenue evaporates?

We knew capitalism required consumption.  
But maybe we didn’t realize it would eventually consume us.

A Different Vision of Justice

Justice doesn’t need to be this complex.  
At its core, it’s simple:  
Don’t harm.  
Don’t exploit.  
Repair when possible.

What if laws were written with humanity in mind?  
What if we prioritized healing?  
What if we saw addiction, poverty, and mental illness not as moral failures, but as calls for care?

Many in prison are not lost causes.  
Some of the wisest, most creative, deeply reflective people I’ve met were locked up.  
Not for who they were, but for what systems they were born into.

The Real Injustice

While millions suffer behind bars, the architects of injustice live unbothered.  
Politicians banning abortion after rape.  
CEOs destroying ecosystems.  
Lobbyists legalizing cruelty.

These are not glitches.  
They’re features of a system designed not to protect us, but to break us.

Our jails resemble third-world conditions more than developed ones.  
Isolation isn’t therapy, it’s punishment.  
It warps the mind, forcing people to become someone else just to survive.  
Then expects them to unlearn it when released.

This is not rehabilitation.  
It's a recursive system.  
A self-sustaining business model, extracting revenue streams from human suffering.

Even state-run rehabilitation often deepens the wounds.

I’ve lived inside that system. During my incarceration, I didn’t see rehabilitation, only dehumanization.

Yes, I deserved consequences for my choices.

But I met people doing more time for missed probation check-ins or nonviolent drug charges.

Not threats - just poor, sick, or stuck.

Cracks in the Illusion

But the cracks are showing.  
People still ask what happened to Epstein’s book.  
The illusion of “law as fairness” is fading.

We’re seeing the game now.  
And that’s the first step to ending it.

Not reform.  
Reimagination.

Strip the noise.  
Center the human.

Make justice what it should’ve been from the start:

- A compass, not a cage.
- A bridge, not a wall.
- A process of making things right, not making things worse.

Let’s build something worthy of the word.
