There's No Need to Apologize

There’s absolutely no need to express regret for what you’ve done—unless you’ve intentionally or unintentionally harmed someone, or occasionally, something. I can’t even count the number of times I’ve apologized to people who were actively trying to harm me—whether they were conscious of it or not often remains unclear.

Many times in these situations. I couldn't grasp why someone would maliciously want to hurt me, and I carried partial responsibility, as if I had done something wrong to warrant this undeserving pain.

Looking back, I realize that most of the times I’ve said “sorry” or apologized to someone, it wasn’t really about them—it was about me. If I’m honest with myself, those apologies often felt like an attempt to seek absolution for the unnecessary guilt I’ve carried since childhood, as though their forgiveness could somehow lighten my own emotional burden.

You're absolutely free to live your life however you choose. But I have a "challenge" for you—one that was given to me a while ago and completely shifted my perspective.

You should try to stop apologizing so much.

I know it’s easier said than done. I’ve been the same way—apologizing for things that weren’t my fault, weren’t my responsibility, or simply didn’t require an apology at all.

For most of my life, I’ve apologized, often to people who didn’t even deserve it. I’ve even instinctively apologized to inanimate objects I’ve bumped into, as though a table or door might take offense. That reflex to apologize was so ingrained, it felt automatic—an almost unconscious habit.

A lot of this stems from my programming as a child, and much of it seems to be constantly reinforced through societal conditioning.

For me, this programming has resulted in a baseline emotion best described as distress. It’s almost humorous to reflect on this now, especially when comparing it to the definition of “sorry”—feeling distress, particularly through sympathy for someone else’s misfortune.

Looking back, it feels as though this distress obscured my deeper, more human emotions, like empathy and sympathy. In a way, it seems this distress manipulated me, forcing me to confront parts of myself I was either unaware of or unwilling to face.

I’ve apologized to people who have directly wronged me, thinking, “Maybe I did something to cause this.” I’ve apologized when things didn’t work out, even when it was entirely out of my control. In truth, it was the universe that probably owed me an apology.

But here's the thing: I don’t like seeing people feel uncomfortable or hurt. Anyone. I’ve even apologized to people going through incredibly difficult situations—“I’m sorry you have to deal with this”—as if I somehow bore the weight of their struggles. And I’d get the same responses: “It’s not your fault, there’s no need to apologize.” Or, sometimes, I’d see the resilience in them shine through: “It’s not such a big deal. It all depends on how you look at things.”

A few years ago, a friend gave me the challenge to stop saying "sorry" unless I truly had something to take responsibility for or genuinely regretted. It was an adjustment. It took weeks of them calling me out every time I reflexively apologized for something I had no control over.

What I learned was transformative: I wasn’t just apologizing out of politeness—I was carrying unnecessary guilt. Guilt that wasn’t mine to hold. I had been conditioned to believe I needed to smooth over every situation, even if it had nothing to do with me.

When I finally stopped apologizing for things beyond my control, it felt like shedding a layer of my old self. I realized I wasn’t responsible for every bump in the road, every misunderstanding, or every difficult circumstance someone else faced. I started holding space for people instead—listening, supporting, and offering understanding without taking on the emotional burden as my own.

So, I challenge you to try the same. Not because apologizing is inherently bad—it’s a valuable part of being human—but because it’s important to know when it’s not necessary. You deserve to feel lighter, to focus on what truly matters, and to free yourself from carrying the weight of things that were never yours to begin with.
