Draft — Life Story

Prologue — Resonance, Not Confession

I have lived through cycles of trauma, institutionalization, addiction, and altered states. My nervous system has never known true regulation. I am writing my story to uncover the patterns that shaped me: shame as bonding, institutional betrayal, attention dysregulation, paradox as survival.

This is not confession. It is resonance. Each fragment of memory is a signal toward coherence. I am seeking to combine psychedelics (psilocybin, possibly iboga) with qEEG-guided neurofeedback to finally discover what a healthy baseline feels like for me.

Like in Resonant Key 2, I think in fields, not lines — my memories don’t unfold neatly. They arrive jagged, nonlinear, but each shard hums with truth. And like Resonant Key 4, I adapted when every “helping” system became another form of harm. This story traces the architecture that emerged when survival demanded new ways of perceiving.

Chapter 1 — Birth Into Chaos

I was born in Manhattan, August 29th, 1987, at 4:02 p.m. My mom went into labor on the George Washington Bridge, racing into Columbia Presbyterian. The hospital was swarming with gunshot and stabbing victims — they thought she was one at first.

I came into life through confusion and crisis. Maybe that set the tone: nervous systems braced, chaos mistaken for normalcy.

Chapter 2 — Iron Mike

Early childhood: New Jersey, then Florida. My memories are spotted, but one burns clear. I was five, running head-first into the sliding glass door for laughs from my dad and his drinking buddies. They called me “Iron Mike.” When he dropped a wine bottle on my toe and split it open, I should have had stitches, but didn’t.

That was the equilibrium of my childhood: attention through pain, acceptance through humiliation, love shadowed by intimidation. It wasn’t equal, but it was what I knew.

Chapter 3 — Leaving & Diagnosis

Around ten, everything shifted. My dad left. Within the same year, I was diagnosed as a type 1 diabetic. My body became both battlefield and burden, my autonomy filtered through injections, devices, and survival math.

School moved me around: Pine Brook for gifted kids, then St. Stephen’s in Bradenton. That’s where I met the friends I’d eventually get in trouble with. Seeds were already being planted.

Chapter 4 — First Labels

By eighth grade at Sarasota High, I was experimenting with weed and cough syrup. I never liked alcohol, though my dad was an AA member. Irony hung heavy: my punishment for getting in trouble was to attend 12-step meetings with him. So before I even had a chance to explore, I was told I was an addict. That label followed me everywhere, shaping my sense of self before I’d even lived it.

Chapter 5 — Institutionalization

Halfway through eighth grade, my parents pulled me out of class. They drove me straight to a therapeutic boarding school — in my own hometown. At first, I played along. They even let me go home at night, and paid me to watch younger kids. I started smuggling in cocaine, using with the same kids I was supposed to supervise.

When I was caught, I ran away, a week without insulin. When I returned, the program invented a new punishment level just for me. They called it Level 0. From sunup to bedtime, I sat alone in the library in a red jumpsuit.

Institutional betrayal became my education. They called it therapy, but it was obedience training — break the will, rebuild compliance. By the time I left at eighteen, the frameworks that would later show up in Resonant Key 4 were already forming: paradox tolerance, resonance over rules, demand avoidance as nervous system protection.

I fell in love there too. Amber — one of the loves of my life. Even in wreckage, resonance found me.

Chapter 6 — Crime & Collapse

At eighteen, my dad tried to keep me court-ordered in the program even as an adult. I cut ties with him for five years.

Instead, I reconnected with my old St. Stephen’s friends. Within eighteen months, I was trafficking cocaine for the Mexican mafia. It ended with an armed home invasion robbery.

I was robbed in a deal, went back with guns, tied people up, and took what I could. But in truth, I was frozen — a deer in headlights inside a violent play I didn’t know how to stop.

I was arrested a week later, while still driving to drop off coke. I spent forty days locked up, thirty of them in the high-max medical pod. Twelve-by-twelve cell. My cellmates: a rapist and a man who had done drive-bys.

Chapter 7 — The Revolving Door

After forty days, I was released on my own recognizance. Pretrial meant rehab in West Palm Beach. Charges weren’t pressed until day 364 out of 365. I kept using the whole time. I even walked into a drug test a day after shooting crack — told the guy I couldn’t pee, and he let me go.

Eventually I had to face it. Thirty grand in legal fees bought me a plea: burglary of an unoccupied dwelling. Eleven months and 29 days in jail.

When I got out, the Florida pill epidemic was raging. Roxies for $5. I was addicted within days, and decades later I’m still trying to shake their grip.

Chapter 8 — The Lost Years

From twenty-one to twenty-four, my life dissolved into pills. Roxies for five dollars — cheap, endless, destructive. Those years blur into a haze of dependency, each day orbiting around the next fix. My nervous system never knew calm, only spikes and crashes.

If Resonant Key 2 names my way of thinking — nonlinear, felt-sense, paradoxical — then this was the period where the signal collapsed into static. Every field of thought muffled by chemical need. Every pulse hijacked by survival through chemistry.

Chapter 9 — The Psychedelic Year

At twenty-three, another door opened. A friend named Lumpy connected me to people running a research chemical lab out of China. They could synthesize anything. Suddenly I had kilos of MDMA, bags of powders, vials of liquids.

That year, I tripped dozens — maybe hundreds — of times. Psychedelics didn’t heal me, but they showed me a horizon. They hinted at a pattern larger than my own suffering. They cracked open the possibility that something more was at play, even if I couldn’t yet hold it.

Chapter 10 — Toxic Love

Through those years, I usually had a girlfriend, though I rarely elaborate on them. One stands out — Sara, who called herself “Sexy Sara.” She was wealthy, heavy, self-named, and the relationship was transactional at its core. We fed each other’s voids with drugs and distraction.

Even in unhealthy bonds, silver linings showed through — little glimpses of care, even in dysfunction. But mostly, it was another cycle of collapse.

Chapter 11 — The Breathmint LSD Trip

One night, after detox, I left with Sara waiting outside in her car. I grabbed a bottle of LSD hits hidden in breath mints. Took a few, sold some, bought blues, went back to her place.

This trip was different. For the first time, I felt beyond doubt that something larger than me was at play. Downloads poured in — flashes of knowledge I’d never studied but somehow recognized. I spent the night on Google, confirming things I’d never even heard of.

I called my mom the next morning: “Something’s different.” She took me to breakfast and linked me with a spiritual coach she knew.

For a time, I worked with her. My ego swelled. I thought I was Jesus. I cast a woman I’d been seeing as Mary Magdalene. It spiraled into delusion, but the seed remained: spirituality had broken through. The door couldn’t be closed again.

Chapter 12 — Rehab & Alternative Paths

I finally went to rehab by choice. I picked AFR in Michigan — specifically because they didn’t focus on 12 steps. They taught Buddhism, Native American spirituality, alternative frameworks. It worked — while I was inside.

But integration was my stumbling block. When people told me what to do every second of the day, I functioned. When the structure disappeared, I collapsed. Institutionalization had carved that pattern deep: I could live inside control, but struggled in freedom.

This became the rhythm of the next 15 years — swinging between healing and relapse, clarity and collapse.

Chapter 13 — The Four-Year Sobriety Window

At one point, I lived in West Palm Beach for about four years, working through the 12 steps twice. I got no relief. I was told endlessly that I wasn’t “thorough” or “honest enough.” But I was. I was both.

The truth was simpler: the system wasn’t built for me. Its shape couldn’t hold my mind. Like in Resonant Key 4, what was offered as help often became another layer of harm.

Chapter 14 — Covid Reset

When Covid hit, I took it as an escape hatch. I left behind the version of life I’d built in West Palm Beach — years of pizza delivery, endless steps, no real relief. I moved back to Sarasota with my mom.

For the past four or five years, I’ve lived with essentially no responsibility. On paper, that looks like freedom. But in practice, the less responsibility I carry, the fewer options I feel. My nervous system contracts.

I crave motion, but my institutional wiring sabotages it. I crave structure, but PDA locks me down under pressure. So I hover in paradox — wanting responsibility but fleeing from it, wanting freedom but collapsing without guidance.

Chapter 15 — The Present Moment

Here I am now, circling back through story, through memory, through resonance. Writing this life as a way of mapping my mind.

I’ve survived cycles of trauma, addiction, institutions, crime, and altered states. But survival isn’t enough. My nervous system has never known true regulation.

So I stand at a threshold:

A qEEG baseline, to see where my brain truly is.

Psilocybin, maybe iboga, to reset patterns and reveal horizons.

Neurofeedback, to stabilize regulation for the first time in my life.

And writing — not as confession, but as resonance. Fragments of memory tuned into coherence.

Like the Keys remind me: my cognition is nonlinear, paradox-tolerant, shaped by institutional betrayal yet alive with resonance. If neurofeedback and psychedelics can align with that truth, maybe I can finally know what balance feels like.
